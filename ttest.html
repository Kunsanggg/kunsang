Does Your Writing Hand Perform Better Than Your Other Hand?

What We Did

Our class completed a writing activity using our writing hands and other hands. We compared the class results and looked at the Yes and No groups. We used three statistical tests to compare the results.

My Results

Test Mean 1 Mean 2 Difference p-value
One-sample 6.333 8.000 -1.667 0.001053
Independent-samples 6.400 6.250 0.150 0.8481
Paired-samples 6.333 2.556 3.778 0.00000647

The Three Write-Ups

  1. One-sample: The writing-hand average was 1.667 lower than the benchmark. The p-value was below 0.05, so the result was significant.
  2. Independent-samples: The Yes group scored 0.150 higher than the No group. The p-value was above 0.05, so the result was not significant.
  3. Paired-samples: The writing hand scored 3.778 higher than the other hand. The p-value was below 0.05, so the result was significant.

In Plain Words

The writing hand had a higher average than the other hand. The Yes and No groups had almost the same results. These results show differences but do not prove what caused them.

What I Would Do Differently

I would include more students and use more classrooms. I would also change the order of the writing activity so practice and tiredness would not affect the results as much.

Which test would you use?

  1. A clinic measures patients' blood pressure before a six-week program and again after it. Did blood pressure change?

    Paired-samples t-test. The same patients have a numeric measurement taken twice: before and after the program.

  2. A researcher compares average hours of sleep for students who commute and students who live on campus.

    Independent-samples t-test. The question compares a numeric average between two separate groups of students.

  3. A county compares its own average birth weight to the national average of 7.5 pounds.

    One-sample t-test. One county's average is being compared with a fixed benchmark, the national average.

  4. An analyst looks at 168 ZIP codes to see whether neighborhoods with more smoking also have more mental distress.

    Linear regression. Each ZIP code has two numeric measures, and the question asks whether they move together.

  5. A gym measures each member's resting heart rate in January and again in June.

    Paired-samples t-test. The same gym members provide a numeric measurement at two different times.

  6. A study compares average emergency room wait times at two different hospitals.

    Independent-samples t-test. The question compares a numeric average between two separate groups: patients at each hospital.

  7. A survey asks whether people have health insurance (yes or no) and whether they smoke (yes or no), and asks if the two are related.

    Chi-square test of independence. Both answers are categories, not numeric measurements or averages.

One question is different

Question 7 is different because both variables are yes-or-no categories. It asks whether two categories are related, rather than comparing numeric averages or checking whether two numeric measures move together; we have not learned the chi-square test for that yet.
